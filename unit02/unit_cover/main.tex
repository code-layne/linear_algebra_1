% ── Unit overview ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blush,colframe=burgundy,boxrule=0.9pt,arc=2mm,
    left=4mm,right=4mm,top=3mm,bottom=3mm]
  \textbf{Unit Overview.}\quad
  Unit 1 asked \emph{which} targets the columns of $A$ can reach. Unit 2 answers the harder
  question: given a reachable target $\bb$, which combination $\xx$ reaches it? The tool is
  \textbf{elimination} --- subtract a multiple of one equation from another until the system is
  triangular, then back-substitute. Students then find that each step is itself a \emph{matrix},
  that undoing a step means \emph{inverting} it, and that collecting the multipliers packages
  the whole process as the factorization $A = LU$. The unit closes with the two repairs that
  make the method work on any matrix: a \textbf{permutation} to move a zero out of a pivot
  position, and the \textbf{transpose}.
\end{tcolorbox}

% ── Big ideas of the unit ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{skillbox}[Big Ideas of Unit 2]{greenbox}
\small
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=3pt, topsep=1pt]
  \item Elimination does not change the answer. Every step replaces the system with an
        \emph{easier system having the same solution} --- so the check is always to rebuild
        $\bb$ from the original $A$.
  \item \textbf{Triangular is easy.} The whole method is one idea: work toward a shape you can
        solve by substitution, one unknown at a time.
  \item Row operations \emph{are} matrices --- $E$ performs a step, $E^{-1}$ undoes it, $\PP$
        reorders the rows --- so a procedure becomes an object you can factor.
  \item $A = LU$ is elimination remembered. Doing the work once means the second right-hand
        side costs almost nothing.
  \item A \textbf{zero pivot} is information, not failure: swap rows if you can, and if no swap
        helps, the matrix is \textbf{singular} --- no solution, or infinitely many.
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}
